01 / FROM PRINT TO MEASUREMENT

A reference frame,
in four straightforward steps.

No special tracking hardware to get started. Just a printed target, a calibrated camera, and a model that matches what you built.

  1. 01

    Print a target

    Print at actual size and mount the whole board on a flat, rigid surface. Measure the reference line before tracking.

    Open the marker board ↗40 mm markers / 20 mm gaps
  2. 02

    Calibrate your camera

    Capture varied views of the checkerboard. Keep focus, zoom and resolution fixed between calibration and tracking.

    Open the checkerboard ↗9 × 6 inner corners / 20 mm squares
  3. 03

    Track the object

    Combine visible markers into one rigid-object pose. Inspect inliers, rejected corners and competing planar solutions.

    Explore the pose API ↗Object → camera / meters
  4. 04

    Use the result

    Stream results into your Python application, or explicitly export CSV for plots, replay and further analysis.

    See the CSV fields ↗Translation / quaternion / quality

03 / MORE THAN DRAWING AN AXIS

Know what your
estimate is built on.

01

Reject bad observations

Corner and whole-marker rejection keeps a coherent majority in the fit, with the exclusions exposed in the result.

02

Make ambiguity visible

Close planar solutions are reported as ambiguous. An explicitly supplied, recent compatible measurement may select a branch.

03

Keep frames and units explicit

Object-to-camera transforms, meter translations and x/y/z/w quaternions. No implicit world frame or invented confidence score.

04 / BUILD SOMETHING WITH IT

A useful building block.
A clear boundary.

Inspect a robot fixture. Follow a handheld target. Give a classroom experiment a visible coordinate frame.

The current toolkit supports camera calibration, rigid marker layouts, pose diagnostics and export. Dodecahedron examples are available for exploring targets with markers on several faces.

ROS 2, docking and landmark-navigation integrations are roadmap work. Real-world accuracy depends on printing, geometry, calibration and imaging conditions; the synthetic replay is a software demonstration, not a precision benchmark.
